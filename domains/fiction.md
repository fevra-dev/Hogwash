# Fiction and Creative Writing

Part of domains/. A different aim from everything else here, not just a different genre: the rest of this skill optimizes for a real claim, clearly made. Fiction optimizes for an experience. That changes which defaults are wrong. "Telling" the reader something is sometimes the correct choice here, not always a violation, see below.

---

## The Stakes Are Current, Not Hypothetical

In March 2026, Hachette pulled the horror novel *Shy Girl* after it was flagged as roughly 78% AI-generated, the first commercially published novel canceled over AI-authorship allegations. Separately, close to 20% of a 14,000-novel sample of self-published Amazon fiction was flagged by Pangram as largely AI-generated, a figure that rose 41% year over year. This isn't a niche concern.

## Narrative Construction, Not Just Prose Style

A 2026 arXiv paper (the STORYSCOPE project, 10,272 prompts, 51,336 AI-generated narratives, released as a public dataset) found that AI-generated stories cluster tightly in a shared region of narrative space, while human-authored stories spread out much more broadly, and that this clustering shows up in the underlying construction of the narrative itself, not just surface prose style. The fiction-specific version of this skill's core bet: structure, here meaning plot and character construction, outlasts any wordlist, the same way it does in prose generally.

## Physical and Sensory Clichés

"She let out a breath she didn't know she was holding" is fiction's version of "results-driven professional," the single most independently-flagged example across current writing-craft sources. "A shiver ran down her spine" is its close cousin.

The mechanism behind why these keep showing up is worth knowing, not just the examples: cognitive research on the availability heuristic suggests writers reach for pre-packaged phrases during drafting because they're cheaper to produce than actually visualizing the specific physical reality of a moment. One useful name for this: a **skipped thought**, a cliché phrase standing in for the harder work of imagining how fear, specifically, uniquely, moves through this specific character's body. That's Specificity Erasure again, just showing up as sensory shorthand instead of a vague statistic.

A related, adjacent tell: weather and setting used as mood shorthand, rain mirroring sadness, a storm arriving with the argument. Fine once. A pattern when every emotional beat gets an atmospheric echo.

Practical test: read the manuscript aloud and flag every smirk, gasp, and lip-bite. Then try cutting the physical beat entirely, if the dialogue alone still carries the emotional weight, the physical tic wasn't doing real work.

## Voice Collapse

The named pattern for what happens to dialogue specifically: different characters converge on the same wording, the same emotional framing, the same instinct to state feelings directly rather than let them surface through subtext. Multiple current sources independently call the result something close to a **therapy intake form**, characters stating their grievances and defending themselves, plainly, with no oblique angle and no subtext.

Worked example, paraphrased from a current craft source. Bad: two characters argue directly about who listens to whom in the relationship. Good: they argue about whether to get a dog, neither one acknowledging that the real fight is about something else entirely, and the piece never states that explicitly either.

One concrete differentiation technique beyond word choice: sentence length and rhythm should vary **between** characters, not just within one narrator's prose. A professor's long, qualifying sentence and a soldier's two-word fragment are doing the same job Sentence Length SD does in core/structural-patterns.md, just applied across a cast instead of across one voice.

## Purple Prose Is Not the Fix for Telling

Overcorrecting into ornate, adjective-stacked description is its own failure, not a solution to under-showing. "The statue felt rough, its aged facade caked with dust and grime as I weighed it in my hand, observing its jagged curves" is worse than "It was heavier than it looked. Some of the orange facade crumbled in my hand." This is the fiction-specific instance of SKILL.md's own core principle: fixing slop badly just swaps one uniformity for another, purple density instead of AI blandness, still not a real choice being made.

## Show Don't Tell Isn't a Binary Rule

Worth stating directly because the common failure mode when correcting it is treating it as absolute: telling is the right call for time bridges ("three weeks passed"), familiar territory the reader already knows, pace control in a fast scene, and deliberate contrast after a long shown passage ("He was dead."). The actual problem isn't telling, it's telling by default, unconsciously, because showing takes more deliberate craft. A writer who can't see their own telling is exactly where outside review earns its keep, the same role this whole skill plays elsewhere.

## Two More Named Patterns, from direct hands-on testing

**Plot repetition**: the tendency to re-explain and restate what already happened, as though the reader (or the model) needs the summary refreshed. **Self-containment syndrome**: each chapter or scene resolves like a complete, standalone unit even when it's supposed to be one fragment of a longer serialized work. This is Every-Paragraph-Resolves from core/structural-patterns.md, showing up one level up, at the scene or chapter level instead of the paragraph level.

## Character Depth vs. Archetype

A weak character relies on surface traits alone, the school bully who's simply evil because the plot needs an obstacle. A developed one has specific insecurities and motivations that can genuinely contradict each other. The tell isn't the archetype itself, stock roles exist in good fiction too, it's whether anything sits underneath the label.

## Screenwriting (a further different medium within this file)

Screenwriting isn't prose, and most of the sentence- and paragraph-level machinery above doesn't map cleanly onto it. Action lines aren't description paragraphs; every line implies a shot, not an image to linger on. Dialogue is behavior, not exposition, doubly so here since Voice Collapse above is, if anything, more visible when a script has to carry character purely through what's said aloud with no interior narration to lean on. Treat this as its own scope note rather than an extension of the prose sections above: load this section, but don't assume the rest of domains/fiction.md's prose-specific advice (sentence rhythm, physical-cliché cuts) applies to action-line writing the same way.

## Workflow Principle

The same sequencing that turned up independently in the LinkedIn and Japanese-language research earlier in this skill's development: write the scene in your own words first, then bring AI in for alternatives or tightening, not as the first draft. This protects the actual creative decision, what the scene needs to accomplish emotionally, from being made by the model by default.
