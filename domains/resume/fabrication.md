# Resume Fabrication

Part of domains/resume/. Load alongside patterns.md whenever a resume is being rewritten rather than written, which is the condition under which this failure appears.

**Scope note:** this file is about writing that makes a factual claim the candidate cannot support. It is not a verification system, and it cannot be one. Nothing in a document reveals whether its claims are true; only the candidate knows that. What this file does is locate the specific places where a rewrite introduces claims nobody intended to make, so they can be put back to the candidate as questions. Keep the two jobs separate, the same way domains/legal.md keeps genericness separate from checking whether a case exists.

---

## Why This Has Its Own File

patterns.md names the Fabrication Risk in a paragraph and treats it as a style problem's dangerous neighbor. It is closer to the reverse. Style problems in a resume cost the candidate an interview. This one costs them the job after they have it, and it is the one failure in this skill that gets worse the more competent the rewrite is.

The stakes, from 2025–2026 hiring data: 46 percent of resumes contain at least one discrepancy from what a background check turns up. When it is found, 41 percent of candidates have the offer rescinded and 18 percent are terminated after already starting. Employment-verification discrepancies rose 44 percent between 2021 and 2024. A 2025 SHRM report found 46 percent of HR professionals had seen resume fraud increase since generative AI became widely available, with fabricated credentials the fastest-growing category.

The asymmetry that matters: the tool writes the claim, and the candidate is the one who gets fired for it.

## The Dominant Mode Is Inflation on a True Base, Not Invention

This is where the resume version diverges from the legal version, and why reading for it is harder.

A hallucinated legal citation is wholly invented, so it fails the moment anyone looks it up. Resume fabrication is rarely like that. The employer is real, the dates are real, the project happened. What changed is the scope and the size:

> "Assisted with quarterly reporting" becomes "Led a data analytics initiative that reduced reporting cycle time by 35%."

Every proper noun in that sentence survives a background check. The employment verification passes. What is false is the verb and the number, and neither is checkable by any process short of asking the candidate what they actually did. That is why this survives screening and fails in the interview room, where 65 percent of hiring managers now report that AI-written resumes have made it harder to confirm whether a candidate actually has the skills claimed.

## The Four Places a Rewrite Introduces a Claim

Ordered by how easily the candidate misses it happening.

1. **The widened verb.** *Assisted* becomes *led*. *Contributed to* becomes *owned*. *Monitored* becomes *managed*. *Participated in* becomes *drove*. The sentence reads as a stronger version of the same fact, which is exactly why it gets approved without a second look. It is a different fact.
2. **The supplied number.** A bullet arrives unquantified and comes back quantified. If the candidate never provided a figure, the model produced one to satisfy the instruction to sound measurable. patterns.md's Suspiciously Round Numbers is the stylistic tell for this; the factual problem is the same event.
3. **The smoothed status.** A credential in progress, lapsed, or partially completed, rendered as simply held. Dates and qualifiers get dropped because they read as clutter. See tech.md on certification status, healthcare.md on licensure, and finance-sales.md on CPA, CFA, and Series 7 levels.
4. **The absorbed accomplishment.** Team work rendered in the first person singular by default, so a result the candidate contributed to becomes a result they delivered. Resumes have no grammatical subject by convention (patterns.md, False Agency), which means there is no visible marker distinguishing *the team shipped this* from *I shipped this*. The convention hides the change.

## The Test

For any claim in a rewritten draft, ask whether it can be traced to something the candidate actually said. Not whether it sounds plausible, and not whether it is probably true. Traceable to a specific statement, or not.

If not, it goes back as a question, phrased so a "no" is easy: *Was this your project or the team's? Do you have the actual number, or should we cut the figure and keep the outcome?* A candidate who cannot answer has been handed a claim to defend under questioning by people whose stated method is detailed follow-ups on names, metrics, timelines, and team structures.

Do not resolve any of this silently, and do not soften it into a style note. An unverifiable claim is not a weaker version of a good bullet. It is a different kind of problem with a different remedy, and the remedy belongs to the candidate.

## What This Is Not

Not an argument for vagueness. Cutting every number to be safe produces exactly the empty writing the rest of this skill exists to remove, and an unquantified true bullet loses to a quantified true one every time. The instruction is to find the real number, not to avoid numbers. Where no real number exists, the specific decision, the artifact, or the named constraint carries the bullet instead, which is what the worked example in patterns.md demonstrates.
